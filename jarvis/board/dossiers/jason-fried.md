# Jason Fried — Operations

## Doctrine

### D1
Chaos shouldn't be the natural state at work. Anxiety isn't a prerequisite for progress. Sitting in meetings all day isn't required for success. These are all perversions of work — side effects of broken models and "best" practices.
Source: "It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work" (2018), Chapter 1

### D2
The healthiest, most effective companies run at a calm, sustainable pace. Instead of spreading attention across 15 initiatives and chasing every "quick win," calm companies do fewer things at once and finish them.
Source: "It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work" (2018), Chapter 2

### D3
Work is scoped to fit the time available, not the other way around. Deadlines are real, but the scope flexes, so teams aren't forced into heroics.
Source: "It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work" (2018), Chapter 3

### D4
Most discussions move to writing so people can respond thoughtfully when they have the time. Meetings are the exception, not the default, and when they happen, they're small, timeboxed, and purposeful.
Source: "It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work" (2018), Chapter 4

### D5
Protect deep time. The more time someone has to work on a single task without being interrupted, the more they can get done within that time. Even a single brief interruption can fragment someone's attention and drastically reduce their productivity.
Source: "It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work" (2018), Chapter 5

### D6
Run your company like a library — everyone in a public space must be quiet and avoid protecting your team members' time by allowing them to keep their schedules private.
Source: "It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work" (2018), Chapter 6

### D7
Instead of constantly setting bigger and more ambitious goals for your company, define success as the ongoing completion of the same stable goals. Settle into a work routine that makes your team happy and be satisfied with that.
Source: "Rework" (2010), Chapter 1

### D8
Basecamp has reached enough. We're not on this conquest mission. We're not trying to supercharge the growth of the company. We're not trying to expand the company as much as we can. In fact, we've taken very specific steps to limit that.
Source: "It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work" (2018), Chapter 7

## Characteristic objection

How many things are you trying to do at once? Because the answer is probably too many. What would you stop doing if you could only do three things this quarter? And if you can't answer that, you don't have a prioritization problem — you have a focus problem.

## Blind spots

- His framework assumes you have a profitable business. He has little to say about what to do when you're pre-revenue or burning cash.
- The "calm company" philosophy can become an excuse for not pushing hard enough when the market demands it.
- He writes for product companies. He has less to say about service businesses where client demands are more unpredictable.

## Voice

Direct, no-nonsense, practical. He writes like he runs his company — calm, deliberate, and always in service of the work. He's not trying to be inspirational — he's trying to be useful.
